Work/Research

Lattice disks

Design and computational evaluation of additively manufactured SS316L disks with internal lattice structures, using the finite element method.

SS316L · L-PBF · ANSYS 2023 R1
Diploma thesis · NTUA · 2024
The question

How does the shape of the internal lattice change the way a 3D printed steel disk carries load?

Five lattice disks, built from three unit cells at different densities, were modelled in Fusion 360, meshed and solved in ANSYS under the same compression, then compared on deformation, strain, stress, strain energy and reaction force.

Three unit cells

At different densities
BCC · 12% and 50%

Body-centred cubic

  • Uniform struts, equal in every direction
  • Nodes keep a fixed orientation relative to each other
  • Nodes treated as rigid; node centres follow an affine displacement field
I-type · 12% and 30%

I-type

  • Structural integrity without nodes
  • Lower squeal propensity in the 4–8 kHz range
  • Simple to design and to manufacture
Tetra · 50%

Tetrahedral

  • Lightweight, with a high strength-to-weight ratio
  • Shear response stays linear up to failure
  • Fails suddenly once shear stress reaches a critical threshold

Method

Model · mesh · load · material
  1. Lattice generationFusion 360

    Each unit cell is patterned across a 90° disk segment and joined to a 1 mm plate above and below.

  2. MeshANSYS

    Plates meshed with 40 angular and 3 axial divisions across the 1 mm thickness. Struts, nodes and fillets get their own face sizing, refined until the maximum deformation changes by less than 0.1% between steps.

  3. Boundary conditionsAfter Barros et al., 2020
    • Radial sides of the segment fixed in x and y
    • Bottom face of the bottom plate fixed
    • Top face of the top plate displaced gradually by 0.5 mm
  4. MaterialStainless steel 316L · L-PBF
    • Proven in laser powder bed fusion
    • Corrosion resistant, for harsh environments
    • Biocompatible, used for implants and surgical tools
    • Strong, ductile and weldable, with few defects in complex shapes

What the numbers say

Findings
  1. Density drives stiffness. Going from 12% to 50% density multiplies the BCC disk's reaction force by 11, from 17.4 to 191.6 kN. The I-type disk doubles from 12% to 30%, from 37.0 to 77.4 kN.
  2. The tetrahedral cell is the stiffest. At 50% it carries the highest reaction force, 246.7 kN, and stores the most strain energy, 100.4 J, about 14 times the force of the BCC 12% disk.
  3. BCC spreads the load most evenly. BCC 12% has the lowest peak von Mises stress, 1,054 MPa, and the lowest average, 307 MPa.
  4. The I-type concentrates stress. It reaches the highest peak stress, 2,118 MPa at 12% density, even though its average stays close to the others.
  5. Same compression, different effort. All five disks deform 0.50–0.52 mm at most, because the load is a fixed displacement. The differences show in the force needed, the stress and the stored energy.
